Every leader I've watched implode had a strategy that still worked. The vision was still clear. The team was still capable. What gave out wasn't the plan — it was the person holding it.

We talk about leadership almost entirely in terms of output: decisions made, systems built, people led well or led poorly. All of that matters. None of it is first. Long before a leader produces anything worth following, they are *becoming* someone — and what they become is a direct function of what's happening in the part of them nobody else can see.

## The soul is not a department

It's tempting to treat your inner life as one more area to manage — schedule it in, optimize it, check the box. Ten minutes of quiet. A verse before the inbox. But a soul isn't a department you can delegate or a metric you can hit and move past. It's the ground everything else grows out of. Neglect it and your leadership doesn't stop; it just stops being sustained by anything real. You start running on reputation, habit, and the fear of letting people down — which will carry you further than you'd expect, and then stop carrying you at all.

I don't think most leaders who burn out or blow up were dishonest. I think they were exhausted in a place they never let anyone see, for long enough that exhaustion became the only voice they were still listening to.

## What an unattended soul sounds like

It rarely announces itself. It shows up as irritability with people who used to have your patience. As a hunger for one more win that never quite satisfies. As isolation dressed up as busyness — you stop letting anyone close enough to ask you a real question. As a growing gap between the person you are in front of a room and the person you are when the room empties out.

None of that means you're disqualified. It means you're due for honesty.

## Practices, not performances

I don't have a five-step framework for soul health, and I'd trust it less if I did. But a few things have held up for me and for leaders I respect:

**Sabbath that actually rests.** Not a day off spent catching up on everything the week didn't allow — a day that puts you back in your place as someone who is loved apart from what you produce. If you can't stop for a day, that's not a scheduling problem.

**A few people who outrank your image management.** Everyone needs at least one relationship where being impressive isn't the goal — where you can say the true, unflattering thing and still be received. Leaders without this don't stay honest for long, including with themselves.

**Prayer that isn't just petition.** Asking God for things is good. But a soul also needs the slower work of just being known — sitting still long enough to remember whose you are before you go be useful to everyone else.

**Regular, unhurried self-examination.** Not self-flagellation. An honest look, on some rhythm, at what's actually going on beneath the calendar. Where am I resentful? Afraid? Performing instead of present? The questions matter more than the answers.

## The order matters

Here's the thing I'd want a younger leader to hear: you cannot organize your way out of a soul problem. You can build better systems, hire better people, sharpen the vision — and if the soul underneath it all is depleted, you will eventually export that depletion into everything you lead. People don't just follow your decisions. They absorb your condition.

So tend the soul first, not because the strategy doesn't matter, but because the strategy was never going to survive an unhealthy person carrying it. Healthy leadership isn't the natural result of better technique. It's the overflow of a life that has somewhere to come from.
